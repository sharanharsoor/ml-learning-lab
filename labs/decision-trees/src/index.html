From surprise to entropy

Picture drawing one random parcel from a node. How surprising would its label be?

Start with inverse probability

Rarer labels give a larger 1/p. For a certain label, p = 1 gives 1/p = 1. We want certainty to contribute zero information.

Take the logarithm

I(p) = log₂(1/p) = −log₂(p). It maps certainty to zero and makes the surprises of independent outcomes add, because their probabilities multiply. For two independent outcomes with probability ½ each, the joint probability is ¼ and the surprise is 2 bits. Base 2 gives the unit.

Change the mix of labels

The starting mix matches all eight training parcels.

Average surprise1 bit

Entropy is the average surprise. If you averaged one surprise per row, a label that appears in four out of eight rows would show up four times. Grouping those rows gives H = −Σ p(label) log₂ p(label). Sum over distinct labels. A label with probability zero contributes zero by the limit of −p log₂ p.

A good question lowers the uncertainty that remains. There’s another way to measure the same label mix, using the chance of a mismatch.

What stays the same

Switch between entropy and Gini while holding a candidate fixed. The same parcels take the same branches. Each criterion uses its own scale to score the label mix in those children.

How thresholds become candidates

Exact search tries a midpoint per gap between distinct weights. Coarse search tries only 2.5, 4.5, and 6.5 here. It sums the label counts on both sides of each edge. Packaging A versus B adds its own candidate in either mode.

What about categorical features and scaling?

The feature Y has values A and B, so one yes-or-no question separates them. With more categories, a binary tree can test membership in a group of categories, or test one category at a time after one-hot encoding. Multiway trees can use more than two branches. Each branch still earns its weight from the same immediate parent.

For an exact numerical search, a strictly increasing rescaling preserves the ordering of the training values and therefore the candidate partitions. The cutoff itself changes units. A tree can also reuse a feature deeper down when a different cutoff still divides the rows.

A mixed child can become a new parent, with its own counts and candidate search. See how the weighted average works deeper in a tree.

Control model complexity

Where should the tree stop?

Add questions when they help on unseen data. A tree underfits when it misses useful patterns. It can overfit when extra questions capture details specific to the training rows.

Compare depths on the same parcels

When growth stops at a node

A pure node can stop. A mixed node can also stop if it reaches the depth limit, can’t give both children the required number of rows, or has no eligible split with enough gain. These rules limit growth during fitting.

How to choose the limit

Compare depth and leaf-size settings using validation data or cross-validation. Keep an independent test set for the final estimate. When you use the six held-out parcels here to choose depth, they serve as validation data.

Pruning is another option. Fit a larger tree, then remove branches whose improvement is too small to justify their added leaves. Tune how much to prune on validation data.

Now look back at the fitted tree and ask how much each feature contributed to the reduction in training impurity.

Attribute the reduction

Give each feature credit

A feature earns credit every time the fitted tree uses it. Multiply the local gain by the share of all training rows that reached the node, then add the credits by feature.

Why multiply by the root share?

Pick one training row at random. It reaches a node of size n with probability n/N, where N is the root size. The split improves the label mix by its local gain for the rows that reach it. Averaged over all rows, its contribution is (n/N) × local gain.

What do all those credits add up to?

Each split replaces the parent’s impurity contribution with its children’s contributions. When you add the reductions, internal nodes cancel. What remains is the root impurity minus the weighted impurity of the final leaves.

Divide each feature’s credit by the total credit to report its share. One strong split near the root can earn more than several small splits. This score describes how this fitted tree reduced impurity on its training data.

See this cutoff as a regression tree

The cutoff search is the same: compare weighted child scores using only the rows in the immediate parent. The mean and MSE are recalculated inside each child. A fitted regression tree predicts one constant per leaf.

Why does the mean minimize squared error?

For any constant prediction c, the average squared error equals MSE at the mean + (c − mean)². That extra square is smallest at zero, which is exactly when c equals the mean. The tree uses that best constant separately in each child while choosing a split.
