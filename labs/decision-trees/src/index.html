Score one node

From surprise to entropy

Return to the eight training emails from the spam filter opener. The root starts with four Legitimate and four Spam. Picture drawing one email at random. How surprising would its label be?

Start with inverse probability

Rarer labels give a larger 1/p. For a certain label, p = 1 gives 1/p = 1. We want certainty to contribute zero information.

Take the logarithm

I(p) = log₂(1/p) = −log₂(p). It maps certainty to zero and makes the surprises of independent outcomes add, because their probabilities multiply. For two independent outcomes with probability ½ each, the joint probability is ¼ and the surprise is 2 bits. Base 2 gives the unit.

Change the mix of labels

The slider starts at the training root’s 50% Spam. Moving it explores other label mixes.

Check the weighting at a 25% Spam share

Set the slider to 25%. Imagine a node with six Legitimate and two Spam emails. Legitimate has probability ¾, so its surprise is about 0.415 bits. Spam has probability ¼, so its surprise is 2 bits. Multiply by the two shares and add. ¾ × 0.415 + ¼ × 2 ≈ 0.811 bits.

The base of the logarithm sets the unit. Base 2 gives bits. The natural logarithm gives nats and rescales every entropy gain by the same positive constant.

Entropy is the average surprise. If you averaged one surprise per row, a label that appears in four out of eight rows would show up four times. Grouping those rows gives H = −Σ p(label) log₂ p(label). Sum over distinct labels. A label with probability zero contributes zero by the limit of −p log₂ p.

In a node with six Legitimate and two Spam emails, which calculation gives entropy?

Show options

What is the right answer?

B. 0.75 × 0.415 + 0.25 × 2

Show explanation

A randomly drawn email is Legitimate with probability 6/8 and Spam with probability 2/8. Weight the surprise by how often each label occurs. The result is about 0.811 bits.

A good question lowers the uncertainty that remains. There’s another way to measure the same label mix, using the chance of a mismatch.

Find the first split

A question puts each of the eight training rows into one child. Score each child, weight it by its share of this parent, then subtract the weighted score from the parent score.

Weighted child impurity = (nyes/nparent) × impurity(Yes) + (nno/nparent) × impurity(No)

The candidate and numeric search controls change this comparison. In Grow the tree, the selected impurity measure carries forward and the fitted tree searches exact midpoints at each node.

What stays the same

Switch between entropy and Gini while holding a candidate fixed. The same emails take the same branches. Each criterion uses its own scale to score the label mix in those children.

How thresholds become candidates

Exact search tries a midpoint per gap between distinct uppercase run lengths. For example, 3 and 4 give 3.5. The coarse demo groups the observed lengths into 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, and 7–8. It tests 2.5, 4.5, and 6.5 by counting labels on both sides. The presence of free gives a candidate in either mode. With entropy, exact search finds 3.5 at about 0.549 bits of gain. With coarse edges, 2.5 and the free indicator tie at about 0.311 bits.

What about categorical features and scaling?

The free indicator Y has values A and B, so one yes-or-no question separates them. With more categories, a binary tree can test membership in a group of categories, or test one category at a time after one-hot encoding. Multiway trees can use more than two branches. Each branch still earns its weight from the same immediate parent.

For an exact numerical search, a strictly increasing rescaling preserves the ordering of the training values and therefore the candidate partitions. The cutoff itself changes units. A tree can also reuse a feature deeper down when a different cutoff still divides the rows.

Control model complexity

Where should the tree stop?

Add questions when they help on unseen data. A tree underfits when it misses useful patterns. It can overfit when extra questions capture details specific to the training rows.

Compare depths on the same emails

Select a depth to update the tree and confusion matrix.

When growth stops at a node

A pure node can stop. A mixed node can also stop if it reaches the depth limit, can’t give both children the required number of rows, or has no eligible split with enough gain. These rules limit growth during fitting.

How to choose the limit

Compare depth and leaf-size settings using validation data or cross-validation. Keep an independent test set for the final estimate. When you use the six held-out emails here to choose depth, they serve as validation data.

Compare the same tree before and after pruning

Cost-complexity pruning compares weighted leaf impurity + α × leaf count. The email tree has three pure leaves and 0 remaining entropy. Removing the free-indicator question leaves two leaves and 0.4512 bits. Keeping just the root leaves one leaf and 1 bit.

Now look back at the fitted tree and ask how much each feature contributed to the reduction in training impurity.

Attribute the reduction

Give each feature credit

Use the spam classifier fitted on the original eight training emails. A feature earns credit each time that tree uses it. Multiply local gain by the share of all training rows that reached the node, then add the credits by feature.

Why multiply by the root share?

Pick one training row at random. It reaches a node of size n with probability n/N, where N is the root size. The split improves the label mix by its local gain for the rows that reach it. Averaged over all rows, its contribution is (n/N) × local gain.

What do all those credits add up to?

Each split replaces the parent’s impurity contribution with its children’s contributions. When you add the reductions, internal nodes cancel. What remains is the root impurity minus the weighted impurity of the final leaves.

Each chosen split’s contribution to feature importance
Chosen splitRoot shareLocal gainCredit

Check what the percentage divides by

In the depth-2 entropy tree, X gets (8/8) × 0.5488 and Y gets (5/8) × 0.7219. Their credits are 0.5488 and 0.4512 bits. Root entropy was 1 bit and the leaves are pure. X’s share is 0.5488 ÷ (0.5488 + 0.4512) ≈ 54.88%.

Divide each feature’s credit by the total credit to report its share. One strong split near the root can earn more than several small splits. This score describes how this fitted tree reduced impurity on its training data.

What if two features measure nearly the same thing?

A split credits the feature the tree actually chose. If capital-run length and another measurement are interchangeable, either one can get most of the credit while the other earns little. Importance describes the fitted model, so it can move when the data, features, or depth change.

Permutation importance asks a different question. After fitting, shuffle one feature across validation rows and check how much the score drops. A feature with a close substitute can have a small drop. Neither calculation establishes that changing a feature would cause the real-world outcome to change.

Why is the leaf mean the best constant for squared loss?

For any prediction c, the average squared error equals MSE at the mean + (c − mean)². At c equal to the mean, the extra square is zero. Using absolute error instead would make a median an optimal constant.

How did the tree compare with a calendar baseline?

The notebook trains on 15,919 hours before November 1, 2012 and evaluates on 1,460 later hours. Both models use clock hour and the working-day flag. The lookup predicts the training mean for each hour and day-type pair.
