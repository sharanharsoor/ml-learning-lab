    An ensemble combines predictions from several models. Each model that contributes is a base learner. Here the base learners are trees.

    Bagging

    Train models separately on resampled rows. Combine their predictions.

    Boosting

    Train models in sequence. Each stage helps correct errors of the ensemble so far.

    Stacking

    Train another model to combine the base models’ predictions. Out-of-fold predictions can train that combiner.

    What changed between the two trees?

    Different rows and repeated counts can change the learned splits. Both trees answer for the same parcel. A missed Review sends through a parcel that needed checking. An unnecessary Review adds work at the desk.

    Draw a bootstrap sample

    Bagging means bootstrap aggregating. Draw a training sample with replacement, fit one model on it, then repeat for the other models and combine their predictions.

    Why give each tree a different tray? A tree’s first question can change when it sees a different mix of rows. Combining several trees gives us a way to reduce how much the final answer depends on any one sample.

      What changes without replacement?

      With 12 draws from 12 rows and no replacement, every row appears once. Drawing fewer than 12 without replacement would give a subset with no repeats. With replacement, repeated and omitted rows can occur even when the number of draws equals the original row count.

      How do we combine the predictions?

      For classification, one simple approach is to count the predicted classes and choose the majority. Another approach averages probabilities by class. For numerical regression, average the predicted numbers.

      Random Forest intuition

      Train three trees and combine their answers

      Each sample trains one decision tree with the same settings. All three trees then answer for the same new parcel. We’ll start with different training rows, then see how a Random Forest also varies the candidate features at each split.

      1. Sample. Draw 12 rows with replacement for each tree.
      2. Fit. Each tree learns its own split questions from the measurements and labels in its tray.
      3. Predict. Give every tree the new parcel’s weight of 5.65 and scan score of 5.7. Follow its answers to a leaf.
      4. Combine. Count class votes or average the class probabilities. The new parcel gets one final route.

      Run the core code

      Change the sampling line, fit one tree, and predict the same parcel. Then run the cell that fits all three trees.

      Open intuition notebook
      rng = np.random.default_rng(bootstrap_seed)
draw = rng.choice(len(X), size=len(X), replace=True)
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(criterion='gini', max_depth=2, random_state=tree_seed)
tree.fit(X.iloc[draw], y.iloc[draw])
tree.predict(parcel)
      Walk through the code line by line
      1. Set the seed. The bootstrap seed is 11. Start a fresh generator with that seed to replay the same draws.
      2. Draw row positions. len(X) is 12, so the positions run from 0 to 11. size=len(X) asks for 12 draws. replace=True makes each position eligible again. The first positions are 1, 1, and 9, which correspond to P2, P2, and P10.
      3. Set up the tree. Gini measures how mixed the labels are. The tree looks for splits that make the child groups less mixed. max_depth=2 allows up to two questions on a path to a leaf. The tree seed of 0 controls random choices during fitting.
      4. Fit the tree. X.iloc[draw] takes the sampled weight and scan-score rows. y.iloc[draw] uses those same positions to take their route labels in the same order. Repeated positions repeat both measurements and label.
      5. Predict the new parcel. tree.predict(parcel) supplies just its two measurements. The fitted tree returns a route.

      Work through the three routes and both final answers

      Tree 1. 5.7 is at most 5.8. The Yes leaf has 8 Auto and 0 Review occurrences, so the tree says Auto.

      Tree 2. 5.7 is greater than 5.25. The No leaf has 0 Auto and 7 Review occurrences, so the tree says Review.

      Hard vote. Two of three trees say Review, for a Review vote share of 66.7%.

      Probability average. The Review shares inside the reached leaves are 0/8, 7/7, and 2/3. Their average is 5/9, or 55.6%. That also gives Review the larger share for this parcel.

      Is vote share the same as average predicted probability?

      Each hard vote contributes one class. In a probability average, a leaf with two Review rows and one Auto row contributes a Review probability of 2/3. For example, three Review probabilities of 0.49, 0.49 and 0.99 give two Auto hard votes but a mean Review probability of about 0.657. In scikit-learn, the forest classifier picks the class with the largest mean probability.

      How do we get different trees?

      Change the rows and the candidate features

      Trees become more diverse when they see different training parcels and consider different ways to split them.

      1. Draw rows for a tree. Its bootstrap sample can include several copies of a parcel.
      2. Draw features at a split. Weight might be eligible at one node and scan score at the next.
      3. Score the eligible questions. The tree uses the rows that reached that node and chooses the question with the largest impurity decrease.

      Rows are resampled per tree. Candidate features are sampled per split. Both changes can reduce how often the trees make the same mistakes.
      Does choosing fewer features always help?

      Fewer candidates can diversify trees. It can also hide a strong feature at an important node. Compare settings on validation data. A feature can be eligible again at another split.

      Where does the 0.3111 Gini decrease come from?

      Gini measures how mixed the Auto and Review labels are at a node. The root has four Auto and eight Review draws, so its Gini is 1 − (4/12)² − (8/12)² ≈ 0.4444. The scan cut leaves four Auto and one Review in the Yes group and seven Review in the No group. Their Gini scores are 0.32 and 0, weighted by 5/12 and 7/12. Subtracting that weighted result from the root score gives 0.4444 − [(5/12 × 0.32) + (7/12 × 0)] ≈ 0.3111.

      Different decisions are useful when the individual models still capture some signal.

      Properties of base learners

      Useful alone, better able to disagree

      A base learner is one of the models that contributes to the ensemble. Each tree should pick up useful patterns. Averaging helps when the trees tend to make different mistakes.

      Change the maximum depth

      Depth caps the number of split questions on a path. All three samples stay fixed as their trees refit.

      The diagram shows Tree 1. The cards report each tree’s prediction for the new parcel and its correct predictions on its own training draws, including repeats.

      Try it. Change depth from 1 to 2 and watch Tree 3. It goes from 10/12 to 11/12 correct training draws, and its call for the same new parcel changes from Auto to Review. Check a held-out set to see how the change affects new parcels.

      How much should one tree learn?

      At depth 0 the tree gives every parcel the same call, based on the majority label in its sample. Extra split questions let it learn an interaction, such as asking about scan score only for heavier parcels. Deeper trees may also pick up details that change from sample to sample. Bagging helps most when averaging can steady those sensitive predictions.

      What if every tree makes the same mistake?

      Every vote points the same way, so the aggregate makes that same mistake. For a numerical prediction, averaging identical numbers also leaves the prediction unchanged.

      Random Forest code implementation

      Fit, tune, and evaluate a forest

      Start with a tree and an untuned forest. Choose settings using only the training set, then evaluate the selected model on unseen parcels.

      1. Split the 1,400 parcels. Use 1,050 for fitting and tuning. Set aside 350 to measure final performance.
      2. Test 15 settings across 4 folds. Within the 1,050, take turns validating on one fold and fitting on the other three. This gives 60 fits for the search.
      3. Refit and evaluate. Take the settings with the best mean validation score, refit on all 1,050, then predict the 350 held-out parcels.

      Implementation notebook

      Run end to end on a larger synthetic parcel dataset with multiple input features.

      Open implementation notebook
      1. Split once. Set aside a stratified test set, which keeps roughly the same class proportions. Keep test labels out of model selection.
      2. Fit both baselines. Train a decision tree and a Random Forest on the same training partition.
      3. Define a search space. Pick plausible list values or distributions for the settings. Decide how many combinations to sample.
      4. Search on the training set. Each candidate fits the training part of each fold and scores its validation part. Choose the best mean validation score.
      5. Evaluate once on the holdout. Refit the selected settings on all training rows. Inspect precision, recall and the confusion matrix on unseen parcels.

      How does randomized search differ from a grid?

      A grid enumerates the specified combinations. Randomized search evaluates a budget of sampled combinations. The search reports the same kind of cross-validation scores for each candidate. RandomizedSearchCV reference

      Why balanced accuracy?

      It averages recall across classes, so both Auto and Review count equally even if their frequencies differ. Review recall answers “Of parcels that needed a check, how many did we flag?” Review precision answers “Of the parcels sent to a person, how many needed a check?” Together they show missed checks and manual workload.

      Cross-validation reserves folds. Bootstrap sampling gives us another source of unused rows, one set for each tree.

      Interview questions

      Explain importance and tune the trade-offs

      Use the same logic from the decision-tree lab when explaining what the forest learned. Then change one setting at a time and reason about the individual trees, their similarity, and the final validation score.

      How does a forest compute feature importance?

      For each split, multiply its impurity decrease by the fraction of the tree’s training draws that reached it. Credit the feature used for that split. Add credits by feature, turn them into shares within each tree, then average the tree shares.

      Work through the numbers for these trees

      In Tree 2, the scan-score split is at the root, so all 12 draws reach it. Its weighted credit is 12/12 × 0.3111 ≈ 0.3111. The weight split is lower down. Its weighted credit is 5/12 × 0.32 ≈ 0.1333.

      Those credits total about 0.4444. Dividing each credit by that total gives 70% scan score and 30% weight within Tree 2. Across the three trees, mean weight importance is (0% + 30% + 64.3%) ÷ 3 ≈ 31.4%. The other 68.6% goes to scan score.

      Impurity-based importance describes how this forest used its inputs during training. Features with many possible split points can get extra credit. Held-out permutation importance asks how much a chosen score falls when a feature’s values are shuffled.

      Why can more trees give diminishing returns?

      More trees stabilize the average for fixed training settings. Similar trees share part of their error, and adding estimators cannot fully average that part away. Check validation gains and compute together.

      How do depth and pruning differ?

      Maximum depth stops growth once a path reaches a specified limit. Minimal cost-complexity pruning removes branches according to the balance of impurity and tree size. A larger ccp_alpha favors simpler trees. Both settings can trade variance for bias.
