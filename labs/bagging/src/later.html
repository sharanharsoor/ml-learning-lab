Variance

Why averaging steadies a prediction

Send the same new parcel through models trained on different batches. A single tree might call it almost certainly Auto on Monday and almost certainly Review on Tuesday. Model variance measures how much the prediction moves when the training data changes.

  1. Keep the parcel fixed. Its weight, scan score and true route stay the same.
  2. Refit both models. Each new training batch supplies data for one tree and a bagged ensemble.
  3. Compare the spread. Look at the Review probability across batches for each model.

Within each batch, a single tree and a bagged ensemble use the same training data. These illustrative probabilities make the spread easy to see.

Work out the spread for the first two batches

Reveal Batch B. The single tree gives Review probabilities of 0.10 and 0.90. Their mean is 0.50. Both estimates are 0.40 away from that mean, so their mean squared distance is 0.16.

The bagged trees give 0.42 and 0.56. Their mean is 0.49. Both estimates are 0.07 away, so their mean squared distance is 0.0049. The square roots, 0.40 and 0.07, are the standard deviations shown as spread.

Across all five batches, both means are 0.50. The bagged probabilities still move less. A model that always gives an Auto parcel a Review probability of 0.80 is perfectly stable. With a 50% decision cutoff it makes the same mistake every time. Check accuracy against recorded routes for held-out parcels.

What if the trees move together?

Compare the variance of the average with the variance of one tree.

One tree
Average of trees

Shared part Part that shrinks

This model assumes equal individual variances and the same correlation for each pair of trees. Think of numeric predictions or class probabilities, then take their average.

Start with 10 trees and correlation 0.30. The shared part is 0.30. Averaging divides the remaining 0.70 by 10, leaving 0.07. Together, that is 37% of one tree's variance. Increase to 100 trees and the ratio falls to 30.7%. The scale measures variance relative to one tree.

Derive the variance of the average

Let B be the number of trees, σ² the variance of each prediction, and ρ the pairwise correlation. The variance of a sum has B variance terms and B(B − 1) ordered covariance terms. Each covariance is ρσ². Dividing by B² for the average gives

Var(average) = [Bσ² + B(B − 1)ρσ²] / B² = σ²[ρ + (1 − ρ)/B]

With three trees, each prediction pairs with itself once, giving three variance terms. The distinct pairs are trees 1 and 2, 1 and 3, and 2 and 3. The sum expansion counts each pair in both orders, giving six covariance terms. That is why three trees give [3σ² + 6ρσ²] / 9.

Set correlation to 0 to see the uncorrelated case. Set it to 1 to see perfectly correlated predictions. Positive correlation leaves a shared part of the variance as the number of trees grows.

Why sample features?

A strong feature can dominate many trees. Giving a node a smaller candidate set lets different useful splits emerge, which can reduce how closely the trees move together.

Can more trees fix bias?

Averaging stabilizes the predictions available from the base learners. Check fit separately. A group of very shallow trees may still miss an interaction in the data.

The model has two jobs now. Grow individual trees and combine their predictions. Put both into a reproducible training workflow.

Out-of-bag scoring

Which trees can evaluate this parcel?

A training parcel is out of bag (OOB) for a tree if it was drawn zero times into that tree's bootstrap sample. Each row gets its own set of eligible trees.

Reveal all OOB predictions

Coverage asks which parcels we could score. O1, O3 and O4 have eligible predictions, so coverage is 3 out of 5.

Accuracy asks how many of those predictions match. O1 and O4 match. O3 was recorded as Review, and its OOB prediction is Auto. That gives 2 correct out of 3 scored parcels, or about 66.7%.

Why roughly 37%?

With n training rows, one uniform draw misses a particular row with probability 1 − 1/n. If a tree takes m independent draws with replacement, the chance it misses that row throughout is (1 − 1/n)m. When m equals n and n is large, this approaches e−1 ≈ 36.8%. Here, with five rows and five draws, the probability is about 32.8%.

What does this score estimate?

OOB uses training rows to approximate performance on unseen data by using trees that missed each row. Reserve a separate held-out test set for final evaluation, especially after using OOB or cross-validation to choose settings. In scikit-learn Random Forest, enabling OOB scoring requires bootstrap sampling.

What if every tree saw the parcel?

There is no eligible OOB prediction for that parcel. This lab reports coverage and scores only rows with predictions. With very few trees, check OOB coverage before interpreting an implementation's score.
