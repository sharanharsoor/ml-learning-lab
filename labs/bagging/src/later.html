Variance

Why averaging steadies a prediction

Imagine we train one tree on a batch of past parcel records, then start over with a fresh batch. We give both trees the same new parcel. A different mix of training rows can change the first split, where the parcel lands, and the chance of Review the tree assigns.

Model variance measures how spread out those predictions are across training runs. In each batch, we’ll also train several trees and average their Review probabilities for that parcel. We can then compare how far the single-tree prediction and the average move from batch to batch.

  1. Keep the parcel fixed. Its weight, scan score and true route stay the same.
  2. Refit both models. Train one tree on the batch. To build the ensemble, draw several bootstrap samples from that same batch and train one tree per sample.
  3. Compare the spread. Look at the Review probability across batches for each model.

Work out the spread for the first two batches

Reveal Batch B. The single tree gives Review probabilities of 0.10 and 0.90. Their mean is 0.50. Both estimates are 0.40 away from that mean, so their mean squared distance is 0.16.

The bagged trees give 0.42 and 0.56. Their mean is 0.49. Both estimates are 0.07 away, so their mean squared distance is 0.0049. The square roots, 0.40 and 0.07, are the standard deviations shown as spread.

Across all five batches, both sets of predictions average 0.50. The bagged predictions sit closer to that average. A steady prediction can still be wrong. If the parcel’s recorded route is Auto and a model always predicts an 80% chance of Review, a 50% cutoff gives the wrong route every time. To measure accuracy, compare predicted routes with recorded routes on held-out parcels.

What if the trees move together?

Suppose one tree’s Review estimate rises 10 percentage points from its usual value and another’s falls 10 points. The changes cancel in the average. When both rise 10 points, their average rises by 10 points too.

Shared part Part that shrinks

Pairwise correlation describes how much two trees’ predictions tend to move together across training runs. On this scale, 0 means their changes have no linear relationship, and 1 means they move in perfect step. The bar treats each tree as equally variable and gives every pair the same correlation.

Start with 10 trees and correlation 0.30. Take one tree’s variance as 100%. The shared 30% stays in the average. Averaging 10 trees divides the remaining 70% by 10, leaving 7%. Together, that is 37% of one tree’s variance. Increase to 100 trees and the ratio falls to 30.7%.

Derive the variance of the average

Let B be the number of trees, σ² the variance of each prediction, and ρ the pairwise correlation. The variance of a sum has B variance terms and B(B − 1) ordered covariance terms. Each covariance is ρσ². Dividing by B² for the average gives

Var(average) = [Bσ² + B(B − 1)ρσ²] / B² = σ²[ρ + (1 − ρ)/B]

With three trees, each prediction pairs with itself once, giving three variance terms. The distinct pairs are trees 1 and 2, 1 and 3, and 2 and 3. The sum expansion counts each pair in both orders, giving six covariance terms. That is why three trees give [3σ² + 6ρσ²] / 9.

Set correlation to 0 to see the uncorrelated case. Set it to 1 to see perfectly correlated predictions. Positive correlation leaves a shared part of the variance as the number of trees grows.

Why sample features?

A strong feature can dominate many trees. Giving a node a smaller candidate set lets different useful splits emerge, which can reduce how closely the trees move together.

Can a stable prediction still be biased?

Suppose heavier parcels with a high scan score need review far more often than other parcels. Trees that can ask only one question may keep underestimating Review for that group. If the average prediction across fresh training batches is still too low, that systematic error is bias. Adding trees can make the average steadier while leaving that error in place.

The model has two jobs now. Grow individual trees and combine their predictions. Put both into a reproducible training workflow.

Out-of-bag scoring

Which trees can evaluate this parcel?

Look for the ticket for parcel O1 in three training trays. Trees 1 and 2 drew it zero times, so they learned their splits using other parcels. We can ask those two trees to predict O1’s route, then compare their joint answer with the route the courier recorded.

We say that O1 is out of bag (OOB) for Trees 1 and 2. The “bag” is one tree’s bootstrap sample. O1 appears in Tree 3’s tray, so Tree 3 is left out of this check. Another parcel may have a different group of trees available.

Coverage asks which parcels we could score. O1, O3 and O4 have eligible predictions, so coverage is 3 out of 5.

Accuracy asks how many of those predictions match. O1 and O4 match. O3 was recorded as Review, and its OOB prediction is Auto. That gives 2 correct out of 3 scored parcels, or about 66.7%.

Why roughly 37%?

With n training rows, one uniform draw misses a particular row with probability 1 − 1/n. If a tree takes m independent draws with replacement, the chance it misses that row throughout is (1 − 1/n)m. When m equals n and n is large, this approaches e−1 ≈ 36.8%. Here, with five rows and five draws, the probability is about 32.8%.

What does this score estimate?

OOB uses training rows to approximate performance on unseen data by using trees that missed each row. Reserve a separate held-out test set for final evaluation, especially after using OOB or cross-validation to choose settings. In scikit-learn Random Forest, enabling OOB scoring requires bootstrap sampling.
