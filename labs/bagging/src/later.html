How do we measure the spread?

Start with one column of scores. Let pi be its Spam score in batch i, and let n be the number of visible batches. A bagged score already averages 25 trees within one batch. The mean below averages that model’s scores across batches.

1. Find the center

Mean = (p₁ + … + pₙ) ÷ n

Add the scores and divide by the number of batches.

2. Measure the spread

Variance = Σ(pᵢ − mean)² ÷ n

Subtract the mean from each score. Squaring keeps distances on either side from cancelling. Add the squared distances and divide by n to describe these batches.

3. Return to the score scale

Standard deviation = √variance

The square root gives a spread in the original units. For a probability score, a standard deviation of 0.12 is 12 percentage points.

Why compare the variances? A smaller variance means the same email’s score is less sensitive to which rows the model trained on. These five batches show stability for E574. To measure classification accuracy, compare predictions with labels across many held-out emails.

Pairwise correlation measures how closely two trees’ scores move together across repeated training runs for the same email. Compare each score with its own tree’s mean. Positive correlation means those deviations tend to point the same way. Zero means there is no linear co-movement. Negative correlation means the deviations tend to point in opposite directions.

Which trees form pairs? With three trees, there are three distinct pairs.

  • Tree 1 + Tree 2
  • Tree 1 + Tree 3
  • Tree 2 + Tree 3

With B trees, each has B − 1 partners. Divide by two because a pair such as Tree 1 with Tree 2 is the same pair as Tree 2 with Tree 1.

Distinct pairs = B(B − 1) ÷ 2

Why does shared variation equal ρ of the baseline? Picture each score as a common swing plus a separate swing for that tree. In this model the separate swings are uncorrelated with one another and with the common swing. The covariance between two scores then comes from the common swing. With equal tree variances, covariance equals ρ × one tree’s variance. That leaves 1 − ρ of the baseline as tree-specific variation.

Why divide the tree-specific part by B? Adding B uncorrelated swings adds their B variances. Averaging divides the sum by B, which divides its variance by B². Together that gives B ÷ B² = 1 ÷ B.

What can we infer? Set correlation to 0.30 and increase the tree count from 10 to 100. The remaining variance falls from 37% to 30.7%, approaching the shared 30%. At 10 trees, lowering correlation to 0.10 gives 19% under the same per-tree variance assumption. More trees shrink the tree-specific part. Lower correlation lowers the floor.

How do we calculate the correlation of a pair?

In each run, subtract each tree’s mean from its score and multiply the two deviations. Average those products across runs to get the covariance. Same-sign deviations contribute positive products. Opposite signs contribute negative products.

Correlation = covariance ÷ (SD of Tree 1 × SD of Tree 2)

Dividing by both standard deviations puts the result on a scale from −1 to +1. Both trees need some variation for a correlation to be defined.

In the moving-together example, both trees deviate by −0.10 in A and +0.10 in B. The covariance is [(−0.10)(−0.10) + (0.10)(0.10)] ÷ 2 = 0.01. Both standard deviations are 0.10, so the correlation is 0.01 ÷ (0.10 × 0.10) = 1.

Derive the variance of the average

Let B be the number of trees, σ² the variance of each tree’s score, and ρ the assumed correlation of every pair. Each tree contributes one variance term. Each distinct pair contributes its covariance twice when the square expands. Each covariance is ρσ² because both trees have standard deviation σ. Averaging multiplies the sum by 1/B, so it multiplies the variance by 1/B².

Var(average) = [Bσ² + B(B − 1)ρσ²] / B² = σ²[ρ + (1 − ρ)/B]

With three trees, each prediction pairs with itself once, giving three variance terms. The distinct pairs are trees 1 and 2, 1 and 3, and 2 and 3. The sum expansion counts each pair in both orders, giving six covariance terms. That is why three trees give [3σ² + 6ρσ²] / 9.

At correlation 0, the variance falls to σ² ÷ B. At correlation 1, it stays at σ². This is why counting trees alone gives an incomplete picture. How their predictions move together also matters.

Why sample features?

If most trees start with dollar-sign frequency, their errors may move together. Offering a smaller candidate set sometimes brings a different useful clue into the tree. The shared part of prediction variance can shrink when the trees make less similar errors.

Can a stable prediction still be biased?

If a model learns that certain workplace words strongly suggest Legitimate mail, it may overestimate that class for an inbox where those words have a different meaning. Even a steady average can have a systematic error, called bias. Compare new predictions with labels from the setting you care about.

Where do bias, variance, and noise show up in a Spam score?

For a probability prediction, square the difference between the predicted Spam score and the recorded label, coded as 0 or 1. This is the contribution to the Brier score. Consider a numerical illustration where emails with identical measurements have a true Spam rate of 0.50. Two equally likely training draws produce estimates of 0.40 and 0.80, with mean 0.60.

Expected squared error = bias² + prediction variance + irreducible noise.

Bias² · 0.01

The mean prediction is 0.60 and the true rate is 0.50. The squared gap is (0.60 − 0.50)².

Variance · 0.04

Both predictions are 0.20 away from their own mean of 0.60. Their average squared distance is 0.04.

Noise · 0.25

Identical measured inputs can still have either recorded label. Both labels are 0.50 from the true mean, so the average squared distance is 0.25.

With an outcome independent of the training draw, these parts sum to 0.30. Averaging can shrink the variance part. A systematic prediction gap or label uncertainty can remain.

Why roughly 37%?

With n training rows, one uniform draw misses a particular row with probability 1 − 1/n. If a tree takes m independent draws with replacement, the chance it misses that row throughout is (1 − 1/n)m. With 12 rows and 12 draws that is about 35.2%. When m equals n and n is large, the limit is e−1 ≈ 36.8%.

When is this score useful?

OOB gives an internal check for bootstrapped models. A tree skipping one row can still see another row with identical measurements, which is why the full notebook keeps matching vectors together for its cross-validation and final test. In scikit-learn Random Forest, OOB scoring requires bootstrap sampling.

What if every tree saw the email?

That email has no eligible OOB prediction. It belongs in the coverage denominator, while accuracy uses only the covered rows. The separate held-out example from the opener can use all three trees because we kept it outside the training pool altogether.
