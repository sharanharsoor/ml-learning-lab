Turn a score into a probability

The model computes z = b + w × x first. The score z can be any real number. The sigmoid maps it onto a probability between 0 and 1 using p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−z). Choose an email or move the feature slider to trace all three steps.

Read the feature in percentage points

One exclamation mark in 200 characters is 0.5%. This model uses x = 0.5 for that rate. Moving from 0.5% to 1.5% adds one percentage point to x.

Read probability and odds separately

If p = 0.75, the odds in favor of Spam are 0.75 ÷ 0.25 = 3, or 3 to 1. The score z is the natural log of those odds, so the odds are ez.

What do the fitted coefficients mean?

The intercept b is the log odds at x = 0. Here b ≈ −0.7957, which gives a fitted Spam probability of about 31.1% with no exclamation marks. Adding one percentage point to x adds about 1.7336 to the log odds and multiplies the odds by e1.7336 ≈ 5.66. The curve bends, so the probability increase depends on where we start. The relationship describes this fitted model and dataset.

Can two emails get the same prediction and have different labels?

Choose E3383 and then E8. Both have 0% exclamation marks and get the same fitted probability of about 31.1%. The recorded label is Legitimate for E3383 and Spam for E8. One measurement can leave emails indistinguishable to this model.

What if the display rounds to 0%, 50% or 100%?

Finite scores give sigmoid probabilities strictly between 0 and 1. The display rounds for reading. The decision compares the full-precision value to the threshold. An exact tie meets the threshold and predicts Spam. A fitted probability describes the model’s estimate. Calibration would check whether similarly scored emails really occur in the predicted proportion.

Fit the curve with log loss

Binary cross-entropy, also called log loss for binary labels, measures how much probability the model gave to the recorded label. For Spam, use p. For Legitimate, use 1 − p. Take the negative natural log of that value, then average across emails.

Loss for one email = −[y × ln(p) + (1 − y) × ln(1 − p)]

Why take the negative logarithm?

Giving the recorded class probability 1 earns a loss of −ln(1) = 0. Giving it probability close to 0 raises the penalty sharply. For independent observations, the probability assigned to all recorded outcomes is the product of their individual recorded-class probabilities. The negative log turns that product into a sum. Minimizing the average of those terms fits the same coefficients as maximizing the product.

How does the fit use all training emails?

Start with b = 0 and w = 0, so every email initially gets a Spam probability of 0.5. The optimizer recomputes the fitted probabilities and updates both coefficients to reduce the average log loss on the training split. Each email contributes p − y to the intercept gradient and x × (p − y) to the weight gradient. The notebook shows the Newton steps used to fit this lesson’s two coefficients. Threshold changes later leave these coefficients fixed.

How does a probability threshold become a cutoff on the input?

First find the score that gives probability t. It is ln(t ÷ (1 − t)). Then solve z = b + w × x for x. At t = 0.50, the score is zero, so the input cutoff is about (0 − (−0.7957)) ÷ 1.7336 ≈ 0.459%. Our weight is positive, so larger inputs meet this cutoff.
