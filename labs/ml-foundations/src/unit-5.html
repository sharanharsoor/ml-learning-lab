Could yesterday’s rentals improve today’s forecast?

Fit a line to predict the number of bikes rented at 8 a.m. on a workday. Move the line yourself, look at its misses, then find out how the fitted line performs on later dates.

Would you keep the forecast at 470 or move it toward 621? Why?

The log recorded 680 rentals on November 1. The calendar forecast was low by 680 − 470 = 210 rentals. Yesterday’s count might help, but this one case can only motivate the idea. Later we’ll measure both rules on the same test workdays.

The two records are 15,904 and 15,928 in the source hour.csv. UCI Bike Sharing dataset

Pair one day with the day before

Feature x

Yesterday’s recorded rental count during 08:00–08:59. In the opening example, x = 621 on October 31.

Target y

Today’s recorded rental count during the same hour. Each target date in this lesson is a workday.

A lag feature is a value from an earlier time. Here “yesterday” means the previous calendar date. A Monday target can use Sunday’s count. If yesterday’s exact 8 a.m. record is absent, this line has no input for that date.

Linear regression turns the input into a numeric prediction using a straight-line rule. We write the predicted value as ŷ and the recorded value as y.

Why is yesterday’s count safe to use at 7:59 a.m.?

Yesterday’s 8 a.m. hour has already ended. Today’s hour has yet to finish, so its recorded count is only used later to score the prediction. We build the training and test rows using the same timing.

What does a missing previous day look like in the source data?

October 31 has a recorded 8 a.m. count of 621. October 30 has no 8 a.m. record. We skip October 31 as a target in the paired evaluation. That same count of 621 can still serve as the input for the November 1 target.

Should we trust the line far outside these observations?

The plotted training inputs span the recorded range. Predicting outside that range is extrapolation. The line keeps its slope even where we have no nearby examples to check it. A negative rental forecast can also happen with an unrestricted straight line. Use the fit within its supported range and check predictions against the real task.

What makes one line fit better?

A line that misses by +20 and −20 has an average signed residual of 0, even though both predictions missed. Squaring the residuals gives 400 and 400. Now every miss increases the total, and larger misses cost more.

MSE = (∑ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²) ÷ n

Mean squared error (MSE) averages one squared residual per row. The index i labels a row. n is the number of rows over which we average. The values below change as you move the line.

Why do unusual observations matter?

A 20-rental mistake contributes 400 rentals². A 200-rental mistake contributes 40,000 rentals². One large miss can pull a least-squares line toward that point. Check whether an extreme observation reflects a real change in demand or a data issue before deciding how to model it.

Fit the line by least squares

Ordinary least squares chooses the slope and intercept that make the sum of squared residuals smallest. On a fixed set of rows, the row count stays constant, so the same line minimizes the mean squared error.

Fit through September 2012

The equation displays rounded coefficients. The fit and score use their full precision.

The slope captures how much today’s rentals tend to change with yesterday’s. The mean of today’s counts sets a useful reference. If we force the slope to zero, the least-squares intercept is that mean.

How do we compute the fitted coefficients?

Let x̄ and ȳ be the training means. For each row, subtract the relevant mean from xᵢ and yᵢ. Multiply those deviations and add the products for the numerator. Square the x deviations and add them for the denominator.

m = ∑ [(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)] ÷ ∑ [(xᵢ − x̄)²]
b = ȳ − m × x̄

For September 17, subtract the training means from the observed pair (128, 683). This gives deviations of about −255.15 and +223.59. Their product contributes about −57,049.05 to the numerator. The first deviation squared contributes about 65,099.05 to the denominator. We sum the products and squares for all training rows before dividing.

Pairs well above both means or below both means contribute positive products. Opposite deviations contribute negative products. Once we know the slope, the intercept makes the line pass through the point made by the two training means, (x̄, ȳ).

Why fit with MSE and report MAE?

Squaring is the fitting rule for ordinary least squares. Mean absolute error reports an average miss in rentals, which is easy to compare and explain. Minimizing training MSE does not guarantee the lowest MAE on later data.

See the same fit in Python 
def fit_line(rows):
    x_mean = mean(x for x, y in rows)
    y_mean = mean(y for x, y in rows)
    cross_products = sum((x - x_mean) * (y - y_mean) for x, y in rows)
    input_squares = sum((x - x_mean) ** 2 for x, y in rows)
    slope = cross_products / input_squares
    intercept = y_mean - slope * x_mean
    return slope, intercept

Each (x, y) pair is yesterday’s count and today’s recorded count. This Colab function computes the training means, adds the terms shown above, and returns the two parameters.

Can a test input come from the day before its target?

Yes. These are forecasts made one day at a time. Yesterday’s observed count is available when we make each new forecast, even if yesterday also belonged to the test period. The line’s fitted coefficients stay fixed throughout that period.
