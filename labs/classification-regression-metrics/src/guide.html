Learning guide

Classification & Regression Metrics

This lab connects an evaluation question to a visible group of cases, a formula, and an executable Python check. Classification asks whether a dispatched order will arrive by an already fixed deadline. Regression estimates its delivery duration. These are separate prediction targets in the same setting.

The core examples use twenty synthetic classification cases and five synthetic regression cases. Frozen scores make every count inspectable; they do not represent a trained production model or calibrated probabilities. An optional UCI Wine Quality notebook adds a fitted-model exercise.

  • Identify TP, FP, FN, and TN with a declared positive class.
  • Explain the denominators of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1.
  • Separate a cutoff decision from score ranking and ROC-AUC.
  • Interpret MAE and RMSE in minutes, and R² relative to a mean reference.
  • Verify browser results in Python and state each metric's limitations.

No previous machine-learning course is required. Useful starting skills are fractions, averages, coordinate axes, and reading a short Python expression. The guide introduces classes, scores, predictions, and residuals before using their notation.

This guide, the instructor workshop notes, and the downloadable resources are public. The presentation layout changes the visible controls; it is not an access restriction. ML Learning Lab source.

Fix the prediction moment and the question

A customer has placed an order and the service has accepted it. The promised arrival deadline is fixed at acceptance, for example 12:40. At dispatch at 12:00, the address, dispatch time, courier location, and current traffic can inform a forecast. Forty minutes remain until the deadline. Arrival at 12:35 is on time; arrival at 12:50 is late.

P = on time, label 1. A predicted P displays On track. N = late, label 0. A predicted N displays Likely late. This is a binary forecast, not an abstention or an order-acceptance decision. Every order is processed, the deadline stays fixed, and a forecast is not a guarantee. Actual arrival later supplies the truth.

The teaching data store only synthetic scores and later outcome labels. They contain no address, traffic, or courier features and demonstrate no trained feature pipeline. Higher scores indicate the intended on-time ordering; a score such as 0.80 is not established as an 80% probability.

An all-on-time forecast is correct on sixteen of twenty deliveries: 80% accuracy. It also gives four incorrect optimistic statuses. That creates a reason to inspect the kinds of errors and the groups used by each metric. No single additional metric answers every operational question.

Six drawing stages

The Board stages are optional reference frames. The core route uses stages 1 and 2 for the opening, then stays in Playground.

  1. Need: twenty outcomes, sixteen on time and four late; 16/20 correctness alongside four incorrect optimistic forecasts. Keep the fixed deadline and prediction moment visible.
  2. Matrix: actual P then N on rows; predicted P then N on columns. Place D01, D14, D19, and D17 before filling all counts.
  3. Denominators: precision selects the predicted-P column; recall selects the actual-P row. Accuracy selects TP and TN as correct outcomes, with all twenty in the denominator. F1 combines precision and recall.
  4. ROC: x=FPR, y=TPR. An actual positive crossed by a descending cutoff moves the point up 1/16; an actual negative moves it right 1/4. AUC describes the fixed score ordering.
  5. R²: compare actual durations, model predictions, and the observed mean 40. Absolute and squared errors connect MAE/RMSE to the squared-error reference.
  6. Recap: match the evaluation question to its group, units, or baseline. Include what the chosen metric leaves out.

In the positive-first matrix the four positions are [[TP,FN],[FP,TN]]. P/N describes the predicted class in a cell name; T/F describes whether that prediction matches the actual class. “Positive” does not mean “correct.”

Formulas, substitutions, and interpretation

Decision metrics

Accuracy = (TP+TN)/(TP+FP+FN+TN). At 0.25 this is (15+3)/20=0.9. It measures overall correctness. The 0.8 all-P baseline demonstrates why class balance and an appropriate baseline matter.

Precision = TP/(TP+FP). Of the predicted on-time cases, 15/(15+1) actually arrived on time. It measures reliability among positive predictions, not coverage of actual positives. With no predicted positives its denominator is zero; a displayed software convention is not evidence of perfect performance.

Recall = TP/(TP+FN). Of the actual on-time cases, 15/(15+1) were predicted on track. It measures positive-class coverage, not reliability among positive predictions. At 0.25 its value happens to equal precision; the two denominators contain different cases.

F1 = 2pr/(p+r) = 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN), where p means precision and r means recall. The count form follows by substituting their definitions and simplifying. At 0.25 it is 30/(30+1+1)=0.9375; at 0.10 it is 32/(32+3+0)=32/35. F1 is a harmonic mean, so a small component pulls it down more strongly than an arithmetic mean. It excludes TN and contains no explicit error costs. When all predictions are N in this dataset, precision is undefined while the count form of F1 is 0.

Lowering the cutoff adds predicted positives. TP and FP cannot decrease on fixed cases, and recall cannot decrease. Precision can rise or fall depending on the added cases. A preferred cutoff depends on the objective, costs, and constraints; it does not follow automatically from the name of a metric.

ROC and AUC

TPR = recall = TP/(TP+FN) is on the vertical axis. FPR = FP/(FP+TN) is on the horizontal axis. FPR starts with actual negatives; it is not precision and is not 1−precision. ROC is not a precision-recall curve.

At 0.25 the point is (1/4,15/16). At 0.10 it is (3/4,1). All-P gives TPR=100% and FPR=100%, while precision is 80%. An all-N decision gives (0,0). Tied scores cross together.

AUC = (positive-case wins + 0.5 × ties)/(actual P × actual N). A positive-case win means an actual-P score is greater than an actual-N score. There are 16×4=64 pairs. Against late cases D14 (0.45), D17 (0.20), D18 (0.15), and D20 (0.05), the positive cases earn 13, 15, 15, and 16 wins: 59 wins, 5 losses, 0 ties. AUC=59/64=0.921875, displayed approximately as 0.922.

The five losses are D15 (0.40), D16 (0.30), and D19 (0.10) against D14 (0.45); and D19 against D17 (0.20) and D18 (0.15). The D20 row earns sixteen positive-case wins because every actual-P score exceeds 0.05. It does not mean D20 itself “wins.”

The selected cutoff changes the operating point and decisions, not these scores or pair results, so AUC stays fixed. Changing a model or its score ordering can change AUC. AUC measures discrimination, not calibration, accuracy at a cutoff, or expected business cost. Class prevalence and the practical relevance of the operating region still matter; precision-recall analysis can complement ROC.

MAE, MSE, RMSE, and R²

A residual is e = y−ŷ: actual duration minus predicted duration. MAE (Mean Absolute Error) = Σ|e|/n, in minutes. MSE (Mean Squared Error) = Σe²/n, in minutes². RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) = √MSE, back in minutes. MAE averages magnitudes; it is not the signed mean residual or bias.

Default residuals are all −5. Thus MAE=5, SSE=125, MSE=25, and RMSE=5. One large miss has residuals [0,0,0,0,−25]: the same MAE 5, but SSE=625, MSE=125, and RMSE=√125≈11.180. Squaring gives large misses more weight. Both have signed mean residual −5; the different RMSE reflects error concentration.

SSE, Sum of Squared Errors, = Σ(y−ŷ)². SST, Total Sum of Squares, = Σ(y−ȳ)². For the five observed durations ȳ=40 and SST=1000 minutes². On the first case, model residual 20−25=−5 gives 25 minutes², while mean-reference deviation 20−40=−20 gives 400 minutes². A residual and its square are different quantities.

R² = 1−SSE/SST. Default: 1−125/1000=0.875. One large miss: 1−625/1000=0.375. Predicting the observed mean 40 gives R²=0, MAE=12, MSE=200, and RMSE≈14.142. Predicting 60 for all cases gives SSE=3000 and R²=−2. Perfect predictions give R²=1.

R² is unitless and can be negative; it is not “87.5% accurate.” Here it compares squared error with the observed evaluation-set mean, the standard R² reference. That mean is explanatory, not a baseline fitted using test labels for deployment. In a predictive workflow fit a baseline on training data and evaluate it separately. With a constant target SST is zero, so the ordinary formula is undefined; libraries may substitute finite values.

Verify in real Python

The browser interaction is a visualization. Python verification is a separate activity in metrics_core.ipynb. The notebook computes counts directly, then compares them with scikit-learn.

  1. Setup and counts: notebook sections 1 to 4 define the fixed cases, expose the accuracy baseline, count the matrix, and choose denominators.
  2. Cutoff experiment: section 5 compares 0.25 and 0.10. Section 6 verifies library results. Section 7 asks which policy suits an explicit objective.
  3. Ranking: section 8 constructs ROC-AUC from the original scores. Passing hard labels to a score-based evaluation discards ranking information.
  4. Duration errors: section 9 verifies MAE, MSE, RMSE, signed mean residual, and R². Compare the equal-MAE default and One large miss examples.
  5. Boundaries and transfer: sections 10 and 11 cover edge cases and exit questions.

The chosen matrix order is P first on both axes. With scikit-learn this means labels=[1,0], yielding [[TP,FN],[FP,TN]]. Do not apply the common label-0-first flattening convention to this matrix.

Check understanding

Can precision be 1 while recall is below 1?

Yes. At cutoff 0.50, all thirteen predicted positives are correct, but three of sixteen actual positives are missed: precision=1 and recall=13/16.

Why do precision and recall both equal 15/16 at 0.25?

They happen to have equal counts here. Precision groups predicted positives (TP+FP); recall groups actual positives (TP+FN). The FP and FN are different cases.

What enters when the cutoff moves from 0.25 to 0.10?

D17 and D18 are actual negatives and D19 is an actual positive. TP rises by one and FP by two. Recall becomes 1; precision becomes 16/19.

Why is AUC unchanged while accuracy changes?

Only the cutoff changed. Score ordering and all sixty-four positive-negative comparisons stayed fixed. Accuracy is evaluated from the selected binary decisions.

Does (FPR, TPR)=(1,1) mean every positive forecast is correct?

No. Both rates use actual-class denominators. In the all-P example precision is 16/20=0.8 despite both ROC coordinates being 1.

What does equal MAE hide in the two regression presets?

Both average five minutes of absolute error. One spreads that error across all five cases; the other concentrates it in a 25-minute miss. RMSE distinguishes them through squared error.

Does R²=−2 indicate an arithmetic failure?

No. SSE is three times SST, so 1−3=−2. Those predictions have more squared error than the observed-mean reference.

Questions and edge cases

Undefined ratios: name the empty denominator, then state the software convention. No predicted positives makes precision undefined; no actual positives makes recall undefined. ROC-AUC needs both actual classes. A finite library output does not change the underlying definition.

Policy versus model: a cutoff selects a decision policy from scores. A score transformation that preserves every ordering and tie preserves empirical ROC-AUC, although numerical cutoffs and calibration may change. Improvements to one metric do not guarantee improvements to another.

Generalization: the fixed examples establish arithmetic, not future reliability. Real evaluation requires representative held-out data, leakage prevention, uncertainty, and a stated operating objective. Class-dependent error costs and prevalence can change the usefulness of a policy.
