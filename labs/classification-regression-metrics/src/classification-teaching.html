When should we say “On track”?

Asha’s delivery desk promised an order by 12:40. At 12:00, when the courier leaves, the app needs to decide what status to show. The model supplies an on-time score. A cutoff turns that score into one of two messages.

  1. Order acceptedFix the promised deadline.
  2. Courier dispatchesScore the order and show a status.
  3. Order arrivesRecord whether it met the deadline.

Follow an order into the matrix

At cutoff 0.25, scores of 0.25 or higher produce On track. A lower score produces Likely late. Positive (P) means on time. Negative (N) means late.

Change the cutoff, keep the scores

Keep the same 20 orders and their recorded outcomes. Compare cutoff 0.25 with a second policy. Only the forecast can change for each order.

Ask a question before choosing a metric

At cutoff 0.25, 15 on-time orders were forecast On track. That same true-positive count belongs to two different groups.

Precision

Of the orders we called On track, how many got there on time?

15 ÷ (15 + 1 FP) = 93.75%

Recall

Of the orders that actually arrived on time, how many did we call On track?

15 ÷ (15 + 1 FN) = 93.75%

Accuracy and F1

Accuracy counts every correct forecast. F1 combines precision and recall and gives a low rate more influence.

Accuracy = (15 + 3) ÷ 20 = 90%

ROC and AUC

One cutoff gives one point. Moving the cutoff traces recall against the false-positive rate. AUC summarizes how the scores rank on-time orders above late orders.

AUC = 59 ÷ 64 ≈ 0.922

Why does the equal percentage hide an important difference?

At 0.25, precision adds D14 to its denominator because we called it On track and it arrived late. Recall adds D19 because it arrived on time and we called it Likely late. The denominators are both 16 here and contain different orders.

Build the decision rule in Python

Start with the same fixed scores and actual labels. This line makes one predicted label per order. Change the cutoff, then count which decisions moved.

cutoff = 0.25
predicted = [int(score >= cutoff) for score in scores]
correct = sum(p == y for p, y in zip(predicted, actual))
accuracy = correct / len(actual)
