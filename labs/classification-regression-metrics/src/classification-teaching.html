When should we say “On track”?

Asha’s delivery desk promises to deliver by 12:40. At 12:00, the courier leaves and the app sends an On track or Likely late message. Accepted orders continue to delivery after either message. When the order arrives, Asha can see whether that forecast was right.

  1. Order acceptedSet the 12:40 deadline.
  2. Courier dispatchesA real model could use the address and current traffic to score the order. The app applies a cutoff to pick the message.
  3. Order arrivesAt or before 12:40 is on time. After 12:40 is late. That observed outcome supplies the label.

We made up 20 delivery records for this lesson. Each row has an order ID, an assigned on-time score from 0 to 1, and an actual on-time or late label for that example. Address, traffic, and the clock times illustrate what a real desk might see. The teaching table starts with the score already assigned.

A higher score ranks an order as more likely to arrive on time. To read 0.45 as a 45% chance, we would first check whether about 45% of orders with similar scores actually arrived on time.

Follow an order into the matrix

Choose one of the 20 past orders. At dispatch, use the assigned score to forecast. After arrival, compare with the recorded outcome. We start at 0.25 so the example shows all four kinds of outcome. A score of 0.25 or higher gives On track. A lower score gives Likely late. P (positive) means on time. N (negative) means late.

From 20 forecasts to four counts

Use the same original cutoff of 0.25 for every past order. Each order adds one count where its actual row meets its forecast column. P means on time and N means late. The same two orders from the replay are shown beside the totals.

Precision

Of the orders we called On track, how many arrived on time? Count the left column. It contains 15 correct forecasts and D14, the false positive.

15 ÷ (15 + 1) = 93.75%

Recall

Of the orders that actually arrived on time, how many did we call On track? Count the top row. It contains the same 15 correct forecasts and D19, the false negative.

15 ÷ (15 + 1) = 93.75%

Accuracy

How many forecasts were correct overall? Add the two correct cells and divide by all 20 orders.

(15 + 3) ÷ 20 = 90%

F1

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. You can calculate it from the counts as twice the true positives divided by twice the true positives plus both kinds of mistake.

(2 × 15) ÷ (2 × 15 + 1 + 1) = 93.75%

Change the cutoff, keep the scores

Keep the same 20 orders and their recorded outcomes. Compare cutoff 0.25 with a second policy. The forecast message is the only thing the cutoff changes. A cutoff of 0.10 includes an order scoring exactly 0.10.

Why try these cutoffs?

0.10 includes D19, which scores exactly 0.10. 0.50 is a stricter rule that gives fewer On track messages. Every score reaches 0.00. The largest score is 0.98, so 1.01 makes every forecast Likely late. The two extreme settings show what happens when everyone gets the same message.

How could the desk choose a cutoff?

For this calculation, suppose the desk assigns 10 penalty points to a false On track message (FP) and 1 to an unnecessary Likely late warning (FN). These weights are an assumption for the example. Add 10 × FP + FN using the counts for each policy.

At 0.25, the total is 10 × 1 + 1 = 11. At 0.10, it is 10 × 3 + 0 = 30. A real desk would agree on its own error costs or constraints, choose the cutoff on validation data, and evaluate that choice on separate test orders.

One cutoff gives one ROC point

Use the confusion counts at 0.25. Of the 4 actual late orders, 1 was forecast On track. That gives the false-positive rate of 1/4, the horizontal coordinate. Of the 16 actual on-time orders, 15 were forecast On track. That gives recall, or true-positive rate, of 15/16, the vertical coordinate.

To build the curve, start above every score so the point is (0, 0). Lower the cutoff to 0.98 and D01, an actual on-time order, enters the On track group. The point moves up by 1/16. At 0.45, D14, an actual late order, enters and the point moves right by 1/4. Orders with tied scores enter together.

Use the original scores to explain the area

Pair each actual on-time order with each actual late order. The pair earns 1 when the on-time score is higher, ½ when the scores tie, and 0 when the late score is higher. For example, on-time D19 (0.10) earns 0 against late D14 (0.45) and earns 1 against late D20 (0.05).

Moving only the cutoff changes the ROC point. The recorded outcomes, scores, and comparisons stay the same, so the area under the curve (AUC) stays the same.
