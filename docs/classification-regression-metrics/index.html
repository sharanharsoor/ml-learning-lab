Classification and Regression Metrics

Should this email go to spam?

An email arrives. A filter can send it to the inbox or move it to a reviewable spam folder. A missed spam message clutters the inbox. A legitimate message in the spam folder might go unread. We need a way to count both mistakes.

  1. 1. Read the email’s featuresUse measurements available on arrival, such as how often the word “free” occurs and the longest run of capital letters.
  2. 2. Score it and choose a folderA fitted model gives a Spam score from 0 to 1. At or above our cutoff goes to the spam folder. Below it goes to the inbox.
  3. 3. Check the recorded labelFor evaluation, open the dataset’s Spam or Legitimate label and compare it with the filter’s decision.

The score comes from a logistic regression model trained on 3,433 labeled emails. Higher scores mean the model ranks the message as more likely to be Spam. We use four decimal places in every comparison. Check calibration on representative data before treating a score such as 0.5177 as a real-world 51.77% chance.

We’ll follow 20 real, held-out Spambase emails, with 10 Spam and 10 Legitimate examples selected across their score ranges. Labels come from UCI’s data. E4130 means row 4,130 in the source data. The folder policy is a teaching scenario. The complete test fold has its own results at the end.

Where the scores and labels come from

Spambase records 57 measurements per email, including word and character frequencies and capital-letter run lengths. We split the 4,601 records into training and test emails, keeping identical feature rows together. We scale the measurements and fit logistic regression on the training rows. Then we score the held-out rows, round each Spam probability to four decimals, and use the published Spam or Legitimate label to evaluate the result. Hopkins et al., Spambase, UCI (1999) · CC BY 4.0

Use the metric buttons to highlight each denominator in the matrix.

Precision

When the filter raises a Spam flag, how often is it right? Read down the Spam prediction column. Of 11 messages flagged, 9 really are Spam and 2 are Legitimate.

TP ÷ (TP + FP) = 9 ÷ 11 ≈ 81.82%

Recall

How much of the Spam did the filter catch? Read across the actual Spam row. Of 10 actual Spam messages, 9 are caught and E35 reaches the inbox.

TP ÷ (TP + FN) = 9 ÷ 10 = 90%

Accuracy

How many emails went to the correct folder overall? Add the two correct cells and divide by all 20 emails. Always predicting the same label would get 10 of 20 right in this balanced selection.

(TP + TN) ÷ 20 = (9 + 8) ÷ 20 = 85%

F1

F1 combines precision and recall into one number. It uses their harmonic mean, which is pulled toward the smaller rate. To calculate it from the matrix, count each true positive twice and each kind of mistake once in the denominator.

2TP ÷ (2TP + FP + FN) = 18 ÷ 21 ≈ 85.71%

Precision and recall share the same 9 caught Spam emails. Precision also counts the 2 false alarms in their prediction column. Recall also counts the 1 missed Spam email in their actual row. If FP and FN happen to match, the two rates match even though the groups answer different questions.

Why use the harmonic mean in F1?

The harmonic mean of precision and recall is 2 × precision × recall ÷ (precision + recall). When precision is 100% and recall is 25%, the arithmetic average is 62.5% and F1 is 40%. F1 rewards a policy that does well at both catching Spam and keeping legitimate mail visible.

How could a mail team choose a cutoff?

Suppose a legitimate message buried in the spam folder costs 10 penalty points, and a missed Spam message costs 1. That is an assumption for this exercise. Use 10 × FP + FN. In the 20 teaching cases, 0.50 costs 10 × 2 + 1 = 21 and 0.75 costs 10 × 1 + 2 = 12. Both cutoffs have 85% accuracy.

A mail team can pick a cutoff on validation data to meet a product requirement or minimize estimated costs. Measure the chosen policy on a separate test set using real class frequencies and error costs.

One cutoff gives one ROC point

ROC stands for receiver operating characteristic. Use one confusion matrix to place one point. At the baseline cutoff of 0.50, FPR = FP ÷ (FP + TN) = 2/10 = 0.20 on the horizontal axis and TPR = TP ÷ (TP + FN) = 9/10 = 0.90 on the vertical axis. FPR asks how many legitimate emails were flagged. TPR is recall, the share of Spam we caught.

If you changed the cutoff in the comparison, the marker starts at that cutoff. Its starting coordinates come from the counts in the second matrix.

Start above every score to get (0, 0). Lower the cutoff to 1.0000 and two tied emails enter together. Spam E1772 moves the point up by 1/10 and Legitimate E4320 moves it right by 1/10. At 0.9998, E613 (Spam) moves the point up by another 1/10. Continue down every distinct score until everyone reaches the spam folder at (1, 1).

Use the original scores to explain the area

AUC means area under the ROC curve. It is also the chance a randomly chosen Spam email outranks a randomly chosen Legitimate one, giving half credit for a tie. E1809 (Spam, 0.5359) beats E4130 (Legitimate, 0.5177). E35 (Spam, 0.0009) loses against E4130. E1772 and E4320 tie at 1.0000 after rounding.

Moving the cutoff picks another point on the same curve. It leaves all the scores and their ranking in place, so AUC stays the same.

One formula can count a big miss differently

MAE treats each missed rental equally. MSE squares each miss before averaging, so an error ten times larger adds a hundred times as much to the total. RMSE brings that squared scale back to rentals. Switch between the actual forecasts and a controlled comparison to see what the metrics measure.

How much squared error did the model save?

For these five hours, compute the model’s sum of squared errors (SSE) and the total sum of squares (SST) against a constant equal to the mean actual count. The mean minimizes squared error among constant guesses on these rows, so it is the reference in the formula for R².

That reference uses the values observed afterward. A constant forecast you could issue beforehand would instead use a mean computed from the training data.

How far off were we per hour?
MAE reports the average absolute miss in rentals. Use it when an extra missed rental costs the same regardless of the miss so far.
Do big misses cause extra trouble?
MSE penalizes large misses more heavily and uses rentals². RMSE applies the same penalty through MSE and reports the result in rentals.
How much squared error did we save?
R² gives the relative reduction versus the evaluation mean. R² is unitless and depends on how much the observed counts vary.
Does the forecast run high or low?
Mean residual shows average direction. Pair it with MAE or RMSE because opposite signs can cancel.

These are system-wide counts. A useful total forecast still leaves station-level planning open. A real planning team would evaluate the hours and stations its decisions affect.

Spam scores and classification metrics

We fit a scaled logistic regression on 3,433 Spambase emails and score 1,168 held-out emails. The lesson selects 10 Spam and 10 Legitimate examples across their score ranges so the matrix and ranking calculations fit on screen. Use the full fold for a broader check of model performance.

Predict Spam for a score at or above the cutoff. At 0.50, the 20 examples give 9 TP, 1 FN, 2 FP, and 8 TN. Rows show the recorded class and columns show the predicted class. A false positive sends legitimate mail to the spam folder. A false negative lets spam reach the inbox.

  • Precision counts correct Spam flags among all Spam flags. TP ÷ (TP + FP) = 9 ÷ 11 ≈ 81.82%.
  • Recall counts caught spam among all recorded spam. TP ÷ (TP + FN) = 9 ÷ 10 = 90%.
  • F1 combines precision and recall. 2TP ÷ (2TP + FP + FN) = 18 ÷ 21 ≈ 85.71%.
  • Accuracy counts all correct decisions. (TP + TN) ÷ total = 17 ÷ 20 = 85%.
  • ROC and AUC compare ranking across cutoffs. ROC plots recall against FP ÷ (FP + TN). On 100 pairs, AUC = (84 wins + 0.5 × 1 tie) ÷ 100 = 0.845.

Use a held-out validation set to choose a cutoff for the costs of those two mistakes. Higher precision can matter when missing a legitimate email has a serious cost. Higher recall can matter when even a few spam emails in the inbox are costly.

Rental counts and regression metrics

The calendar model averages past Capital Bikeshare counts for the same hour and workday status. For 08:00 on November 1, 2012, it forecast 470 rentals and the network recorded 680. With residual defined as actual minus forecast, the miss is +210 rentals. A positive residual means the model forecast too low.

The five worked hours give residuals 131, 210, 93, 12, and 13 rentals.

  • MAE averages the absolute residuals. 459 ÷ 5 = 91.8 rentals.
  • MSE averages the squared residuals. 70,223 ÷ 5 = 14,044.6 rentals².
  • RMSE returns the square-rooted MSE to the count scale. √14,044.6 ≈ 118.51 rentals.
  • R² compares squared error with the evaluation mean. With mean 349.4, SST = 188,175.2 and R² = 1 − 70,223 ÷ 188,175.2 ≈ 0.627.

The evaluation mean defines the standard R² denominator. A forecast made before the hour can use a separate baseline computed from training data. The notebook and lesson report that baseline for the full chronological test period.
